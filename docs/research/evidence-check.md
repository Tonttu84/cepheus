# Evidence check: relapse-prevention concept after TMS

Checked 2026-11-13 with web search. Sources were read at abstract level, through search-result abstracts, repository pages or Europe PMC records. Full texts were not read unless noted. PubMed pages refused automated fetches, so PubMed links come from search results and Europe PMC; the IDs are correct but were not opened page by page. "No source found" means this run found nothing, not that nothing exists.

Verdict scale: **supported**, **partly supported** (the core holds but some details are overstated), **contested** (real evidence on both sides), **unsupported** (no source found, or the evidence goes against it).

---

## 1. Slowing recovery (critical slowing down) as an early warning

**Verdict: partly supported. The concept is sound, but sensitivity for individuals is low.**

Evidence:
- **Group level.** van de Leemput et al. (PNAS 2014) found that rising autocorrelation, variance and correlation between emotions go with a higher chance of switching between depressed and normal states.
- **Single case.** Wichers & Groot (2016) followed one person for 239 days with up to 10 diary reports a day (1,474 observations) while an antidepressant was tapered. Autocorrelation and variance, computed in moving 30-day windows, rose before a transition around day 127. The letter gives no exact lead time in weeks, so the claim "weeks before relapse" is not stated as such.
- **Replications.**
  - Smit et al. (2022 preprint, later published) followed 37 people coming off antidepressants. Warning signals came before recurrence in only **32.9%** of them. Variance rose more in people who relapsed; autocorrelation did not.
  - Schreuder et al. (J Consult Clin Psychol 2024) followed 41 remitted adults, 22 of whom had a recurrence. Warnings based on rising standard deviation preceded 23–36% of recurrences, with 0–16% false alarms.
  - Curtiss et al. (Psychol Med 2023, n=31): only rising autocorrelation predicted worsening (r=0.41). Variance and network connectivity did not.
- **Review.** Helmich et al. (2021) review the challenges and recommendations for early-warning methods in psychopathology. This run did not read it in detail.

Sources:
- van de Leemput IA et al. 2014, PNAS. https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/critical-slowing-down-as-early-warning-for-the-onset-and-terminat/
- Wichers M, Groot PC et al. 2016, Psychother Psychosom 85(2):114. https://doi.org/10.1159/000441458
- Smit AC et al. 2022, "Critical slowing down in momentary affect as early warning signal…", PsyArXiv. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/u6vdg (published version: https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/critical-slowing-down-in-momentary-affect-as-early-warning-signal/)
- Schreuder MJ et al. 2024, J Consult Clin Psychol 92(8):505. https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000871
- Curtiss JE et al. 2023, Psychol Med 53(7):3124. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291721005183
- Helmich MA et al. 2021, "Early warning signals and critical transitions in psychopathology: challenges and recommendations". https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/early-warning-signals-and-critical-transitions-in-psychopathology/

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Rising variance and autocorrelation in daily mood have preceded some depressive recurrences, but in prospective studies they catch only about a quarter to a third of them, so we treat them as one input to a personal model, not as an alarm."

## 2. Rumination: abstract "why" versus concrete "how"

**Verdict: partly supported. Rumination as a risk factor is solid. The claim that it predicts better than other strategies is overstated, and concreteness training has small, mixed trial evidence.**

Evidence:
- **Onset versus duration.** Nolen-Hoeksema et al. (Perspect Psychol Sci 2008) conclude that rumination predicts the *onset* of depression more consistently than its *duration*. Duration is predicted when rumination interacts with negative cognitive styles. So "onset and duration" is too strong as written.
- **Compared with other strategies.** Aldao et al. (Clin Psychol Rev 2010; 114 studies, 241 effect sizes) found that maladaptive strategies, rumination among them, had medium to large links with psychopathology. Adaptive strategies had small to medium links. This run did not confirm that rumination is the single strongest strategy.
- **Abstract versus concrete thinking.** Watkins (Psychol Bull 2008) reviews when repetitive thought is constructive and when it is unconstructive. The level of construal (abstract versus concrete) is one of three factors the review names.
- **Concreteness training trials.**
  - Watkins et al. 2009, in 60 people with low mood: concreteness training reduced symptoms and rumination compared with waitlist.
  - Watkins et al. (Psychol Med 2012), an RCT in 121 people with major depression: guided self-help concreteness training **did not beat relaxation training on symptoms**, but did reduce rumination and overgeneralisation.

Sources:
- Nolen-Hoeksema S et al. 2008, "Rethinking Rumination", Perspect Psychol Sci. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26158958/
- Aldao A et al. 2010, Clin Psychol Rev. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2832862 (the search result points here; not opened)
- Watkins ER 2008, Psychol Bull 134(2):163. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.163 (full text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2672052/)
- Watkins ER et al. 2009, "Concreteness training reduces dysphoria", J Abnorm Psychol. https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/11163
- Watkins ER et al. 2012, Psychol Med, guided self-help concreteness training RCT. https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0033291711002480

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Rumination is a well-established risk factor for depression onset, and Watkins' work suggests that steering people from abstract 'why' thinking to concrete 'what next' thinking reduces rumination, although in the one trial it was no better than relaxation for symptoms."

## 3. Dormant vulnerability (cognitive reactivity) and MBCT

**Verdict: partly supported. MBCT is solid. Cognitive reactivity as a predictor is contested. The claim that it is stronger with more episodes was not confirmed as a moderator of MBCT.**

Evidence:
- **Cognitive reactivity predicts relapse, in some studies.** Segal et al. (Arch Gen Psychiatry 2006) gave 54 recovered patients a sad-mood induction. The rise in dysfunctional attitudes predicted relapse over 18 months.
- **Other studies disagree.** A 2012 study of cognitive therapy responders found no cognitive reactivity effect. Dysfunctional attitudes measured without a mood induction predicted relapse instead.
- **Link to MBCT.** Kuyken et al. (2010) found that after MBCT, reactivity no longer predicted relapse. This is consistent with MBCT "decoupling" reactivity from relapse, but it is one study.
- **MBCT efficacy.** The individual-patient-data meta-analysis by Kuyken et al. (JAMA Psychiatry 2016; 9 RCTs, 1,258 patients) found:
  - MBCT lowered relapse risk over 60 weeks compared with no MBCT (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.58–0.82).
  - It also beat active treatments, including antidepressants (HR 0.79, 0.64–0.97).
  - Number of previous episodes and age at onset did **not** moderate the effect. Higher residual symptoms went with a larger benefit.
  - Most of the trials enrolled people with three or more episodes (for example the PREVENT trial).

Sources:
- Segal ZV et al. 2006, Arch Gen Psychiatry. https://cih.ucsd.edu/sites/cih.ucsd.edu/files/mbpti/Cognitive%20Reactivity%20to%20Sad%20Mood%20Provocation%20and%20the%20prediction%20to%20depressive%20relapse.pdf
- 2012 study of cognitive therapy responders. https://utsouthwestern.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/cognitive-reactivity-dysfunctional-attitudes-and-depressive-relap
- Kuyken W et al. 2016, JAMA Psychiatry 73(6):565. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.0076 (Europe PMC record, PMID 27119968)

**How to say it in a pitch:** "MBCT cuts relapse risk by roughly 30% compared with usual care in recurrent depression, and works best for people with residual symptoms. One proposed mechanism is breaking the link between a dip in mood and the return of old thinking patterns."

## 4. Distraction

**Verdict: partly supported for short-term relief. The avoidance objection is plausible, but this run found no source for it.**

Evidence:
- **Short term.** In mood-induction experiments (Nolen-Hoeksema & Morrow 1993; Lyubomirsky et al. 1998), people already in a low mood who ruminated felt worse and solved problems worse than those who were distracted. The effect appears only in people who are already dysphoric.
- **Comparison with mindfulness.** One study found that distraction lifted low mood relative to rumination, but less than mindfulness meditation did.
- **Definition.** Nolen-Hoeksema defined distraction as turning attention to pleasant or neutral thoughts and activities.
- **Avoidance objection.** No source found in this run for long-term harm from distraction used as avoidance. Section 12c covers the related idea of experiential avoidance.

Sources:
- Lyubomirsky S, Caldwell ND, Nolen-Hoeksema S 1998, J Pers Soc Psychol. https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Lyubomirsky-Caldwell-Nolen-Hoeksema-1998.pdf
- Review citing Nolen-Hoeksema & Morrow 1993 and the mindfulness comparison. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2672047

**How to say it in a pitch:** "In lab studies, shifting attention outward interrupts rumination and lifts low mood in the short term, so we use it as a brief circuit-breaker that leads into concrete action, not as a way to avoid feelings."

## 5. Passive sensing

**Verdict: partly supported. Correlates exist, but accuracy figures are inflated by small samples and lack of external validation. Relapse prediction is still early.**

Evidence:
- **Wearable AI meta-analysis** (npj Digital Medicine 2023, 54 studies). The pooled *highest* accuracy was 0.89, but the pooled *lowest* accuracy was 0.70. The authors say the field is "in its infancy" and not ready for clinical use.
- **Phone GPS correlates.**
  - Saeb et al. (JMIR 2015, n=28 with usable data): circadian movement (r=−0.63), location entropy and location variance (r≈−0.58) correlated with depression scores.
  - The PeerJ 2016 replication (48 students) found weaker correlations (r≈−0.43 to −0.46).
- **Personal models.** A 2025 JMIR review of 9 studies reports accuracy of 79–91%, higher with personalised or multimodal models. This supports the idea of comparing a person against their own baseline.
- **Relapse prediction specifically.**
  - RADAR-MDD (623 people, up to 2 years) reported data availability and links between Fitbit sleep variability and outcomes. This run found no headline relapse-prediction accuracy from it.
  - A 2026 McMaster study in JAMA Psychiatry (93 remitted adults) linked a flatter day–night activity difference to later relapse. This run saw only news coverage of it, and no AUC.
  - Physiology:
    - Minaeva et al. (Sci Rep 2024): actigraphy change points appeared near the transition in 69% of people tapering antidepressants.
    - George et al.: heart-rate-interval trends showed no consistent rise before recurrence.
- **HRV.** No specific source was checked in this run for "lower HRV in depression".

Sources:
- npj Digital Medicine 2023 wearable AI meta-analysis. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10163239/
- Saeb S et al. 2015, JMIR 17(7):e175. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4526997
- Saeb S et al. 2016, PeerJ. https://peerj.com/articles/2537
- JMIR 2025 review of longitudinal sensing. https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e57418
- RADAR-MDD protocol and retention. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6379954/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8860359/
- Minaeva O et al. 2024, Sci Rep 14:855. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50960-1
- George SV et al. 2022 preprint. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/gk8rd
- McMaster 2026 coverage (secondary only). https://www.icthealth.org/news/wearables-offer-early-warning-of-depression-relapse

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Phone and wearable signals such as mobility, sleep regularity and rest–activity rhythm track depressive symptoms modestly at group level, and the best results so far come from personal models. Relapse prediction from passive data alone has not been validated yet."

## 6. Self-report as ground truth: burden and adherence

**Verdict: supported. Adherence is around 80% in studies, and the number of prompts did not clearly drive dropout.**

Evidence:
- **EMA compliance meta-analysis.** Wrzus & Neubauer (Assessment 2023; 477 articles, N≈677k) found a typical design of 6 prompts a day for 7 days, with 79% compliance. The number of prompts did not predict compliance or dropout. Financial incentives raised compliance.
- **Other compliance figures.**
  - Williams et al. (JMIR 2021): 81.9% compliance overall. Links to burden were inconsistent.
  - Jones et al. (Addiction 2019): below 80% in substance users.
- **RADAR-MDD** (multi-year study):
  - 80% completion of the main outcome questionnaires.
  - About 20% withdrew.
  - 44.6% of those who stopped answering surveys after 8 weeks still shared passive Fitbit data. This is an argument for combining passive and active data.
- Note: these are research settings, often paid. Real-world app retention is likely lower; this run found no source with a figure for that.

Sources:
- Wrzus C, Neubauer AB 2023, Assessment 30(3). https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1839595345
- Jones A et al. 2019, Addiction. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6492133/
- RADAR-MDD retention, Eur Psychiatry 2022. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8860359/

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Short daily check-ins reach about 80% compliance in studies, and long cohorts show that passive data keeps flowing when people stop answering, so we use brief self-report to calibrate and passive data to fill the gaps."

## 8. After TMS specifically

**Verdict: supported for relapse rates. Maintenance TMS is weak and mostly uncontrolled. The DLPFC–sgACC finding is supported but debated.**

Evidence:
- **How often depression returns.** Senova et al. (Brain Stimulation 2019; 19 studies) pooled data on people who responded to TMS. Response was sustained in:
  - 66.5% at 3 months (n≈732)
  - 52.9% at 6 months
  - 46.3% at 12 months (9 studies, n=247)

  So **about half lose response within a year**. Maintenance treatment and a higher proportion of women predicted better durability.
- **Predictors of relapse.** A systematic review (2019) found few studies and inconsistent predictors.
- **Maintenance TMS.**
  - A 2021 "preservation TMS" review found 4 RCTs (1 sham-controlled), 14 open trials and 12 case series, with widely varying protocols.
  - A 2023 review in J Pers Med reports that relapse risk peaks after about 5 months.
  - This run found no source on psychotherapy or digital tools used specifically after TMS.
- **DLPFC–sgACC target.**
  - Fox et al. (2012): DLPFC sites that worked better were more anticorrelated with the subgenual cingulate.
  - The finding has been replicated in independent data, and personalised targeting methods followed (Cash et al., Hum Brain Mapp 2021).
  - A 2024 paper notes that controversy remains (Elbau et al. 2023).
- **Default mode network.** Liston et al. (Biol Psychiatry 2014; 17 patients, 35 controls): TMS normalised subgenual hyperconnectivity within the DMN. Baseline sgACC connectivity predicted improvement. The sample was small.

Sources:

- Miljevic A et al. 2019, J Affect Disord, relapse predictors. https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/potential-predictors-of-depressive-relapse-following-repetitive-t/
- Wilson S et al. 2022, J Affect Disord, preservation TMS. https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/systematic-review-of-preservation-tms-that-includes-continuation-/

- Fox MD et al. 2012, Biol Psychiatry. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4120275 (search result; bibliographic entry https://bibbase.org/network/publication/fox-buckner-white-greicius-pascualleone-efficacyoftranscranialmagneticstimulationtargetsfordepressionisrelatedtointrinsicfunctionalconnectivitywiththesubgenualcingulate-2012)

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Of people who respond to TMS, only about half are still responding a year later, and maintenance protocols rest mostly on open-label data, which leaves a clear gap for low-cost monitoring and support between clinic visits."

## 9. Anhedonia as a residual symptom

**Verdict: supported, with the caveat that whether it is a state or a trait is unresolved.**

Evidence:
- Several reviews state that anhedonia often persists after other symptoms remit.
- Reward deficits are seen in fully remitted patients, and may be part of an underlying vulnerability (CNS Spectrums review).
- A 2019 study of unmedicated remitted patients with recurrent depression found impaired reward-learning signals in the ventral tegmental area (VTA).
- Anhedonia predicted slower remission in adolescents in the TORDIA trial.
- Reviews split anhedonia into liking, wanting and learning. Whether it is a state or a trait is not settled.

Sources:
- Reward systems and cognitions in MDD, CNS Spectrums. https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/93CB160988D85EC3AB37B8A1DD5A5815/S1092852918001335a.pdf/reward-systems-and-cognitions-in-major-depressive-disorder.pdf
- Reviews returned by the search on residual anhedonia. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6368373 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6734943

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Anhedonia, reflecting reward-system dysfunction, often outlasts low mood, so we track enjoyment and motivation separately from mood rather than assuming one score captures recovery."

## 10. Relapse signature and allostasis

**Verdict: partly supported. Early-warning-sign plans exist, but the trial evidence is mainly in psychosis. "Allostatic load" is a real term, though it is not the standard clinical word for staying well.**

Evidence:
- **Relapse plans.** A Cochrane review of early-warning-sign interventions in schizophrenia (Morriss et al.) found:
  - fewer relapses than usual care (23% versus 43%), but with very low quality evidence
  - fewer relapses and rehospitalisations, but no change in time to recurrence
  - an unclear contribution of the early-warning-sign training itself, since it was combined with other psychological interventions

  This run found **no systematic review for depression-specific relapse signatures**.
- **Allostasis.** McEwen defines allostasis as "achieving stability through change". Allostatic load is the wear and tear from chronic or poorly regulated stress responses, and McEwen links it to depression. It is an accepted stress-physiology term, but it describes the *cost* of adaptation, not "actively maintained balance" in a clinical relapse-prevention sense.

Sources:
- Morriss R et al., Cochrane CD005147. https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD005147.pub2/abstract/ja
- Gleeson JF et al., 2024 Clin Psychol Rev, early-warning signs of psychotic relapse. https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/items/336fd2d0-4c96-4723-94ef-75d929ac2139
- McEwen BS 2008, Eur J Pharmacol. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2474765

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Personal early-warning-sign plans are established practice in relapse prevention, best studied in psychosis, and we digitise that practice for depression."

Avoid presenting "allostasis" as the clinical term for staying well. Use "relapse prevention" or "maintenance of remission" instead.

## 11. Personality

**Verdict: partly supported. Neuroticism and self-criticism are solid. The matching hypothesis is contested. Trait-matched interventions are untested.**

Evidence:
- **Neuroticism.**
  - Kotov et al. (Psychol Bull 2010; 175 studies) found it the strongest correlate across diagnoses, but the data are cross-sectional.
  - Jeronimus et al. (Psychol Med 2016; 59 prospective studies, N=443k) found a large prospective link (d=0.50–0.70) that **halves** after adjusting for baseline symptoms and psychiatric history (d=0.10–0.40).
- **Self-criticism.**
  - Löw et al. (2020), a meta-analysis of 49 studies with 3,277 patients: pre-treatment self-criticism goes with poorer psychotherapy outcome (r=−0.20).
  - One CBT study found that self-critical perfectionism, but not dependency, predicted minimal improvement.
  - A 2025 systematic review of perfectionism in RCTs found mixed evidence with a high risk of bias.
- **Matching (congruency) hypothesis.** This run found **no meta-analysis**. Primary studies are mixed:
  - Sociotropy combined with interpersonal stress has the most consistent support.
  - Autonomy and self-criticism combined with achievement stress is inconsistent, and sometimes reversed.
  - Results depend on whether full-scale scores or subscales are used.
  - Some argue that these traits may be consequences of depression rather than causes.
- **Trait-matched treatment.**
  - Compassion-focused therapy reduces self-criticism (meta-analyses 2023: g≈0.40 across 17 studies; small numbers of studies).
  - Treatments that target intolerance of uncertainty reduce worry (Wilson et al., J Anxiety Disord 2023: g≈1.3 for intolerance of uncertainty; the advantage over general CBT was not maintained at follow-up).
  - **No source found** that tests matching these treatments to personality type.

Sources:
- Kotov R et al. 2010, Psychol Bull. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658315
- Jeronimus BF et al. 2016, Psychol Med. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27523506/
- Self-criticism and psychotherapy outcome meta-analysis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31864153/
- Perfectionism in RCTs, systematic review (Shafran et al.). https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10214606/
- Congruency primary studies: Iacoviello et al. https://mijn.bsl.nl/cognitive-personality-characteristics-impact-the-course-of-depre/532690 and the late-life study https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2834205
- CFT and self-criticism meta-analysis 2023, Br J Clin Psychol. https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/handle/2445/216627
- Wilson EJ et al. 2023, J Anxiety Disord, intolerance-of-uncertainty treatment. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37271039/

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Neuroticism and self-criticism are established risk factors, and self-criticism predicts poorer therapy response, so the app can offer compassion-focused exercises to self-critical users. We present trait-based tailoring as a hypothesis to test, not an established finding."

## 12. Struggle and paralysis

**(a) Depressive thinking as logical reasoning on biased premises. Verdict: unsupported as stated, and the evidence that exists points against it.** This run found no source showing that depressed people reason correctly from biased premises. What it found goes the other way:
- Depressed mood impairs syllogistic reasoning, especially on conflict problems, and high working memory buffers the effect (Wasielewski et al., Front Psychol 2021, n=115 students).
- The "depressive realism" effect is negligible (Moore & Fresco, Clin Psychol Rev 2012; 75 studies, d=−0.07).
- Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.645751 and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22717337/

**(b) Effort, reward and helplessness. Verdict: partly supported.**
- Effort-based decision-making is reduced in depression, but the deficit is small. Pillny et al. (Psychol Bull 2026), a preregistered meta-analysis, found g=−0.28 in major depression, smaller than in schizophrenia or bipolar disorder.
- One computational study points to a lower bias towards accepting effort, not changed sensitivity to effort or reward (Valton et al., Psychol Med 2025).
- **Learned helplessness has been revised.** Maier & Seligman (Psychol Rev 2016) argue that passivity is the *default* response, and that what is learned is control, through prefrontal circuitry. The evidence is from animals.
- "Executive load of the inner struggle": no source found.
- Sources: Pillny 2026, doi:10.1037/bul0000510 (https://ifp.nyu.edu/2026/journal-article-abstracts/bul0000510/); Valton 2025, doi:10.1017/S0033291725101967; Maier & Seligman 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4920136

**(c) ACT and experiential avoidance. Verdict: partly supported.**
- Experiential avoidance correlates with depressive symptoms (r≈0.56) and with major depression (r≈0.45). Akbari et al. (2022; 441 studies) is correlational, so it does not show cause.
- ACT compared with traditional CBT (Fang & Ding 2023; 38 RCTs): CBT was slightly better for anxiety. This run could not extract the pooled depression comparison.
- Sources: https://contextualscience.org/publications/akbari_seydavi_hosseini_krafft_levin_2022 and https://contextualscience.org/publications/fang_ding_2023

**(d) Action before motivation; very small actions. Verdict: partly supported.**
- Behavioural activation as a whole is supported (see section 7).
- A single structured BA session showed effects (Gawrysiak et al. 2009).
- Online micro-interventions give immediate mood gains that do not last (J Technol Behav Sci 2023).
- No trial of "two-minute actions" as such was found.
- Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41347-023-00314-5 and https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/service-delivery/a-brief-behavioral-activation-treatment-for-depression--a-randomized-p/

**(e) Delay in starting activity as a measurable sign. Verdict: unsupported for the specific markers; related signals exist.**
- No source found for time from waking to first movement, or for unanswered messages.
- Related signals that do have support:
  - lower mobility and location variance (Saeb 2015/2016, section 5)
  - blunted rest–activity rhythm before relapse (McMaster 2026, secondary coverage)
  - actigraphy change points (Minaeva 2024)

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Depression lowers the willingness to spend effort for reward, so the app proposes the smallest possible next action instead of waiting for motivation. Behavioural activation supports this; micro-step versions are still being tested."

Drop the claim that depressive thinking is logical.

## 13. The product as a trigger

**(a) Orthosomnia. Verdict: partly supported. It is a real concern but rests on case reports.**
- Baron et al. (J Clin Sleep Med 2017, 13(2):351) described patients whose preoccupation with tracker data worsened their sleep.
- Orthosomnia is not a recognised disorder, and the evidence is a small case series.
- Source: https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-tracking/orthosomnia (PMID 27855740)

**(b) Streaks and scores. Verdict: partly supported. The evidence is weak but points one way.**
- A consumer-research study, reported secondhand, found that breaking a streak goes with quitting the platform.
- A British Journal of Health Psychology analysis of about 59,000 posts on X about fitness apps found themes of blame and shame. It sampled negative posts, so it cannot show how common harm is.
- For perfectionistic users specifically, only practitioner commentary was found.
- Sources: https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification and https://studyfinds.com/fitness-app-motivation-study-myfitnesspal/ (secondary summaries)

**(c) Self-monitoring and worry or rumination. Verdict: contested or unknown. No evidence of harm was found, and some evidence suggests neutral to small benefit.**
- The MoodMonitor RCT protocol (BMC Psychiatry 2016) expected a small *positive* effect, citing Kramer et al. (benefit at 7 weeks, gone by 6 months). This run did not find its results.
- In ECoWeB-PREVENT, a self-monitoring-only arm was the comparator. Only the CBT app beat it.
- On showing versus hiding raw data from users: **no source found**.
- Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5075187/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9886135/

**Design rules (no streaks; show only what the user can act on).** These are reasonable precautions that follow from (a) and (b), but no trial was found that tests them directly. Present them as design principles, not as evidence-based findings.

**How to say it in a pitch:** "Case reports of sleep-tracker anxiety and evidence that broken streaks lead to dropout led us to avoid streaks and raw scores. We show the user only what they can act on, and we would test that choice."
