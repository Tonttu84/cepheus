considered and dropped by the owner. Anything asked of an unwell person should cost
almost no effort (`AGENTS.md`), and our research found the effort deficit in depression
to be small (`research/2-mental-measures.md`).

## 1. A small reward for answering, and a rewarded walk on other days

### What the clinician said

Reward treatment works at least for people with stimulant addiction or antisocial
traits; the clinician does not know if it has been studied in depression. In Finland,
depending on the clinic, such rewards are tax-wise and bureaucratically impossible, at
least in the public sector.

The clinician thought the rewarded activity a good idea, but did not know what the
reward should be. Walking is a very good default, but many people have barriers to it,
from fear of public places to chronic pain, so an "own choice" alternative should be
offered.

### The proposal

**What changed (14 Nov 2026):**
- The walk becomes a walk by default, with an activity the person chooses themself as an
  equal alternative.
- Sponsoring is added as an open option to explore working around public-sector payment
  limits.

**For answering.**

- Each question round in which the person responds earns a small amount, for example
  1 euro of gift-card value. When 10 are collected the person gets a gift card of a
  kind they chose, such as food delivery or a sports membership.
- The reward is for responding at all, including "Not today". Nobody is paid to report
  a particular mood, the honest way out stays free, and only true silence goes unpaid.
- The reward never depends on what the answers say.

**For a walk or chosen activity, on other days.**

- The questions come twice a week. On two other days of the week the app offers a short
  walk by default, or an activity the person chooses themself as an equal alternative,
  with its own small reward of the same size.
- The two are independent. Answering earns its reward the moment the person responds,
  whether or not they ever do the activity. The activity earns its reward whether or not
  they answered. Neither is shown as unfinished because of the other.
- This follows the owner's condition: a person who has no energy for both must not end
  up skipping both.
- Leaving the activity costs nothing and is not counted against the person.
- We cannot see whether someone did the activity. The reward follows from the person's
  own tap that they did it, so it is taken on trust.

**For both.**

- The count never resets and is never shown as a run of days or as progress to defend.
- The clinic hands out the card. The app holds no payment details.
- Sponsoring as an open option: on 14 Nov 2026 the owner suggested exploring whether an
  outside sponsor could pay for the rewards, to work around public-sector bureaucracy.
  This raises its own open questions: who the sponsor is, ensuring the sponsor learns
  nothing about the patients (it must not), and whether having a sponsor changes the
  tax position (not checked).

### Why

- People get bored and stop answering, also when they are well. The plan treats silence
  as a warning, so every well person who stops costs a nurse's call. A reward should
  keep them answering, which makes silence easier to read. Of the reward for answering
  alone, the owner said that two euros a week is little against the cost of health
  care, and that paying patients is likely to be frowned upon.
- People feel less bound to a system than to a person who calls them. A small reward is
  meant to make up some of that.
- The answering rate our plan leans on already comes from paid study participants:
  `research/5-products-and-gap.md` calls about 80% "the paid-study ceiling, not
  real-world use".
- A hypothesis: being active in any small way may itself help a person who has become
  passive, and a small reward might train a useful habit. The owner expects the benefit
  to be marginal. The product makes no treatment claim. A hypothesis may be stated
  together with the way to test it: the system stores every answer next to what
  happened afterwards (plan principle 6 and D11), so patients who were offered a reward
  can later be compared with those who were not.

### How it meets our own rules

`AGENTS.md` says no streaks, no scores, and that a missed answer is never punished.
"Collect until you have 10" is a counter, and a round with no response earns nothing.
The proposal keeps to the rules by rewarding "Not today" as well, by never resetting
the count, and by never showing it as a run to defend. Whether that is enough is for
you to judge.

The plan also says the questions come twice a week and no more, because frequent
prompts wear people out (D9). The walk adds two more prompts a week. They are offers,
not questions, and can be left at no cost, but it is four contacts a week where the
plan has two.

### Which activity

Which activity is open. The owner named a walk as worth considering, or another
activity whose effect is better confirmed. The proposal is written with a walk; the
table shows what `research/3-state-shifters.md` found for it and for the alternatives:

| Activity | What that report found | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| A single bout of exercise or a walk | Affect improves after one bout, SMD 0.585 (18 studies), in depressed people (Bourke 2022) | Medium: it means leaving the house |
| Brief mindful self-focus, or distraction | Reduced induced negative mood, that is, a low mood brought on in a laboratory, in depressed patients (n=76, Huffziger & Kühner). Not shown for a dip that arises by itself | Low |
| Slow breathing | Improved positive affect over a month of daily practice; healthy volunteers only | Low |
| Bright light, single session | Mood improved after a first session; depressed people, mostly with seasonal depression | None |

### Cost

Up to 4 euros a week for each patient, so about 200 euros a year if every reward is
earned. This is our own arithmetic, not a study.

### Risks

- Answers given carelessly, just to collect.
- Paying patients in ordinary care may be unacceptable, culturally or by the rules of
  the health service. Not checked for Finland.
- A reward could change what silence means in ways we have not foreseen. The rule's
  thresholds would need checking again once it is in use.
- Rewarding a walk is closer to treatment than rewarding an answer.
- Four contacts a week may tire people where two did not.

### Questions for the mentors

1. Would a clinic be allowed, and willing, to give patients a small reward for
   answering? (One clinician notes that in Finland rewards are impossible for tax and
   bureaucratic reasons, at least in the public sector; could an outside sponsor or
   private clinic work around this?)
2. Is an activity the person chooses themself an effective alternative to a walk, and
   what kind of reward would be suitable?
3. How would you test the hypothesis that a small reward for being active helps, and
   what would count as a result?

## 2. Adjusting for other conditions

### What the clinician said

Anxiety is indeed the most common questionnaire combined with depression
questionnaires. Anxiety therefore stays the proposed pilot, now with this remark as
support.

On a swing into mania after TMS: a person in mania is usually not worried about it
themselves, so questions would have to come from other people; of course if it is not a
severe situation one notices it oneself too. Also, TMS has not been studied for
bipolar disorder and should not be used for it, so the situation is very marginal.

### The proposal

Many people with serious mental conditions have other mental or physical conditions as
well, and these may get in the way of detecting depression. Two steps, the first one
small:

1. **A personal threshold, set by the clinic.** For a patient whose usual PHQ-9 is high
   for a known reason, the clinic marks this at enrolment. For that patient the rule
   warns on a rise from their own baseline, not on the fixed total of 10. This needs no
   new question and a small, tested change to the rule, whose thresholds already sit in
   one versioned configuration.
2. **One further questionnaire, only for patients who have that condition.** As a
   pilot, with a single other condition. The clinic switches it on for a patient.
   Nobody else is asked anything more, so the owner's earlier rule of asking no extra
   question holds for everyone else. The questionnaires are already stored as data, so
   a second one is a data file and a switch for each patient.

### Why

The baseline is taken straight after the course, when the clinic judges that the
treatment has worked (`questions.md`, on another open trail). For most patients that
gives a baseline below 10. The warning rule then warns when a complete PHQ-9 totals 10
or more. Three of its nine questions are about sleep, energy and appetite. Our
reasoning, not checked against a source: long-term pain, poor sleep or a physical
illness can raise those answers too, so a person with such a condition may score above
10 even straight after a successful course. That person would raise a warning every two
weeks, and the nurse would learn to ignore it.

Which condition to pilot is open. Anxiety is one candidate. Not checked, and to be
confirmed before anything is built: how common it is beside depression in people
treated with TMS; and whether the anxiety questionnaire we have in mind (the GAD-7) is
free to use, comes from the same source as the PHQ-9 and has the same answer options.

### Risks

- More questions for the people who already carry the most.
- A second questionnaire needs its own baseline and its own rule for what counts as a
  change. Those would be untested starting values, like the ones we have.
- More conditions mean more combinations to get wrong. One pilot condition, not several.

### Questions for the mentors

1. Which other conditions are most common in your TMS patients, and which of them most
   often confuse the picture? (Anxiety is supported by the clinician as the most common
   paired questionnaire.)
2. Would a clinic set a personal threshold for a patient, and who would decide it?

(The question on whether a swing into mania after TMS needs its own question was removed.
The clinician noted that people in mania are rarely concerned about it themselves, so
self-report does not work well, and that TMS is not studied or approved for bipolar
disorder, making the situation very marginal.)

## 3. Tell the clinic something: a message read by a person

### The proposal

The patient can always send the clinic a short message about something the questions
did not ask, not only on question days. A person at the clinic reads every message.
Software never reads, scores, sorts or summarises the text, and no AI takes part. It is
a safety net: a patient who wanted help is not missed because the questionnaire did not
ask the right thing.

**Next to the message box, always:**

- The crisis path.
- One plain sentence that the message is read by a person when the clinic is open, and
  that for anything urgent the person should call the emergency number or the crisis
  line now.

**What happens to a message:**

- It appears on the clinic's screen as its own item, apart from flags and crisis
  messages.
- It stays on the clinic's screen until a person at the clinic marks it as read.
- The patient sees that the message was sent and when it was read.
- A message never changes a round, a score or a warning.

**A first step that needs no text:**

- A button "I would like someone to contact me", which raises the same kind of item on
  the clinic's screen with no text.
- This button can be built first; the text box follows.

### Why

- A fixed questionnaire only hears what it asks about. The owner, 14 Nov 2026: we
  should have the option to tell something that was not asked.
- Handing free text to humans serves as a safety net, making sure the clinic does not
  miss a patient who wanted help because the questionnaire did not ask the right thing.

### How it meets our own rules

- **Reversing the question set's rule:** The question set says "No free text in the
  must-have build" (`questions.md`, on open trail 10). This proposal reverses that line,
  so if approved it needs a decision row in `decisions.md` that names what it replaces.
- **Automated decisions and crisis:** `AGENTS.md` rules that no AI decides alerts, and
  nothing automated stands between a person in crisis and a human. This proposal keeps
  both rules: a person reads every message, and the crisis path is always beside the
  message box.
- **Handling private health data:** Free-text messages are health data and the most
  identifying data the system would hold, because people write names, places and details
  of other people into free text. To protect privacy:
  - A strict length limit.
  - Stored only for the clinic of that patient.
  - Readable only by that clinic's staff and the patient.
  - Never read or processed by the system's rule or by any analysis.
  - Never included in the dataset used to check the rule (the third layer of agreement).
  - Deleted when the patient's data is deleted.
  - Every reading by clinic staff is logged.

### Risks

- A message sent at night or at a weekend waits until the clinic opens, so the
  urgent-help sentence and the crisis path must be impossible to miss.
- A clinic may receive more messages than it has staff to read, which is a staffing
  question for the clinic.
- A patient may expect a reply that does not come, so the clinic must say clearly how it
  answers.

### Questions for the mentors

1. Would a clinic take on reading such messages, who would read them, and how fast?
2. Would a clinic rather start with the "contact me" button only, without free text?

### Option: an AI marks messages that may be urgent

This is an open option inside proposal 3 that the team can approve or reject on its
own. It is not decided.

**The owner's idea and reasoning (14 Nov 2026):**

The owner suggested scanning messages with AI for priority, in his words: "We could scan
it with AI for priority. It is just an additional safety layer, even if a mistake is
being made and it is not flagged then without it, the message would also have waited
until next day etc to be read". The reasoning: it only adds a layer. A message the AI
misses is no worse off than without it.

**How it would have to work for that reasoning to hold:**

- **Raise-only:** The AI may only raise a message. It can mark one "may be urgent, read
  now". It can never lower, hide, delay, sort down or summarise a message.
- **Order and human review:** Every message is still shown to the clinic in the order it
  arrived, marked or not, and read by a person. No mark means nothing.
- **Patient view:** The patient is not told that a message was or was not marked. The
  crisis path and the urgent-help sentence stay beside the message box whatever the AI
  does.
- **Local processing:** The text never leaves our system. The model runs on our own
  server, not an outside AI service.

**The owner's decision on the rule:**

`AGENTS.md`, "Rules of the problem", says a general AI model "must not decide when to
alert a clinician".

The owner's decision, open to the team like his other decisions, in his words (14 Nov
2026): "It is fine, we override the rule for this. This is a safety vault and our basic
rules dont apply to safety vaults", and "this is just a demo not the end product". For
this raise-only mark on free-text messages, and only for it, the owner sets that rule
aside, because a mark can only add attention and never takes any away.

It needs a decision row in `docs/decisions.md` that names the rule it sets aside. That
row is added when this proposal is accepted. Every other rule still applies to the
messages. A person reads every one, the crisis path is always beside the box, and no AI
triggers or holds back a warning in the question flow.

**Concerns to flag:**

These concerns do not block the demo, but must be settled before real use:

- **Server security:** The text is the most sensitive data the system would hold. The
  model runs on our own server, never an outside AI service, and the server holding it
  must be secured accordingly. The owner (14 Nov 2026): "We just need to decide that the
  server needs to be safe, flag it as concern".
- **Automation bias:** Once staff see marks, an unmarked message may start to feel safe
  to leave until later, so a miss can be worse than with no AI at all. That would break
  the owner's reasoning. The clinic would need to keep reading every message in order.
- **Medical device classification:** Sorting messages by urgency is a clinical function
  and could make the software a medical device (decision D14 avoids such claims). Not
  checked.

**Questions for the mentors:**

1. Would a clinic want such a mark?
2. Would staff trust unmarked messages less carefully?

## Points for the question set and the plan

Remarks from the clinician and open proposals that bear on other open trails, recorded
here as points for the team without changing those files:

- **PHQ-9 threshold:** The PHQ-9's severity bands are 5 points wide, so a rise of 5
  moves a person into the next band; the clinician asked whether 5 would be more
  logical than the rule's 6 (`questions.md`, open trail 10). The 6 comes from research
  on a reliable change in one person; choosing between 5 and 6 is for the team to
  decide.
- **Baseline definition:** The baseline is not easy to define, a known problem even in
  diagnosis when one should know a lifetime baseline. The clinician noted it should
  hardly be longer than about two weeks, and that the person may already have improved
  during the TMS course.
- **Varying questions twice a week:** The clinician suggested the questions could vary,
  sampling different topics. This pulls against using the validated instruments
  unchanged and asking nothing outside the curated set, and is for the team to weigh.
- **Who makes the follow-up call:** Whether a clinic can make a nurse's call within
  about a week depends on the clinic and may be somewhat random. Acute clinics can do
  it, but the neuromodulation clinic itself is unlikely to make follow-up calls. Who
  makes the nurse's call is an open point for the plan.
- **Free text:** Proposal 3 would change the question set's line on free text ("No free
  text in the must-have build", `questions.md`, open trail 10) by letting a patient send
  a short message read only by clinic staff.

## A first literature check, not verified

An automated search was run on both proposals on 14 Nov 2026. It returned search
summaries only: no paper was read, most study sizes are missing, and an earlier search
of the same kind contained an error. Nothing below may be used in the product or the
pitch until someone has read the sources. It is listed so that the right questions get
asked.

- Paid studies of repeated phone questionnaires are reported to reach higher answering
  rates than unpaid ones.
- Rewards are reported to raise careless answering in some studies.
- Small rewards for a target behaviour are reported to work well in addiction care; the
  usual objections are that it looks like bribery and may crowd out a person's own
  motivation.
- European and Finnish ethics guidance is reported to turn on "undue inducement"; a
  token reward is said to fall below it, without a source that settles it for
  ordinary care.
- Fixed small rewards are reported to keep people answering better than a lottery.
- Other conditions are reported to be very common among people treated with TMS for
  depression, anxiety and long-term pain among them.
- Physical illness and pain are reported to raise the PHQ-9's questions on sleep, energy
  and appetite.

## Not checked

- Whether small rewards raise answering among people with depression, and whether they
  change how honestly people answer. (The clinician noted rewards work in addiction and
  antisocial traits, but did not know if studied in depression.)
- Whether paying patients for answering is permitted in Finnish health care. (One
  clinician stated rewards are impossible for tax and bureaucratic reasons in the public
  sector; whether an outside sponsor or private clinic changes the tax position is not
  checked.)
- How common other conditions are among people treated with TMS, and how much they
  raise PHQ-9 totals. (The clinician confirmed anxiety is the most commonly paired
  questionnaire, and that TMS is not studied for bipolar disorder.)
- Which questionnaire is best for a second condition, and its terms of use. (Anxiety
  stays the proposed pilot; specific terms of use remain to be checked.)
